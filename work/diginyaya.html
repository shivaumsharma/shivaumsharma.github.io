Case study · Multi-agent AI · Legal tech

DigiNyaya

A prototype that drafts resolutions for routine civil disputes with five coordinated AI agents, and is built to know which cases it must hand to a human.

Role
Engineer and product owner: agents, retrieval, evaluation, CI and cloud
When
2026, still in active development
Stack
Python, FastAPI, React + TypeScript, PostgreSQL, SSE, AWS + Terraform
Status
Prototype with no live users; evaluated on archived judgments

The problem

India's district courts carry a backlog of crores of pending cases, and for a routine claim the cost of counsel can exceed the amount in dispute. So a whole class of disputes is never filed at all.

The product question was not "can an LLM write a judgment?" It was: which disputes can a machine handle safely, how do you show your reasoning, and how do you stop it from ever inventing money, deadlines or citations?

What I built

Five coordinated agents (ingestion, precedent research, argument analysis, mediation, resolution drafting) run through a hand-rolled orchestration state machine that routes on confidence, loops back to research when precedent coverage is thin, and pauses for the mediation decision.

A two-checkpoint safety gate sends weak, out-of-scope or contested cases to a human reviewer before the AI can decide. Resolutions stream over Server-Sent Events from a durable, hash-chained event log, so a page refresh never loses or duplicates a run.

Decisions that mattered

  1. Put deterministic guardrails around every model output

    The model proposes; code disposes. Relief amounts are clamped to a band derived from real precedents, dismissal is forced whenever the respondent's case is at least as strong as the claimant's, and citations are filtered to what was actually retrieved. Hallucinated citations are blocked before they reach a user.

  2. Hide the winnability score from the respondent

    Claimants see a 0 to 100 case-strength score before filing. Showing the same score to respondents let them coast on a low-effort reply because the AI had "already decided" the case was weak, so respondents see the evidence review but not the score.

  3. Evaluate against real judgments, and retire numbers that did not hold up

    My first figures (a “50% to 67%” improvement) came from a pilot of 46 judgments. I scaled the evaluation to 23,841 archived district-court judgments, treated that pilot as superseded, and now publish the larger-set numbers, including the unflattering ones.

Results

  • 23,841archived district-court judgments evaluated
  • 65.3%resolved without escalation; 34.7% went to a human
  • 56.3%full match with the real court, of 15,573 judged cases (was 39.4% before an amount-award fix)
  • 75.1%winner accuracy, below the 83.1% claimant-always-wins baseline

Full match means the right winner and a relief amount within ±20% of the real award. Scored by an LLM judge that has not yet been validated against human labels, on archived eCourts and Indian Kanoon open data. The amount-award fix was designed after seeing this data, so treat the jump as in-sample. Macro-F1 is 0.634 versus 0.454 for the baseline. The weakest area is small-claims debt recovery (47.5% full match, 7,549 cases). There are no live users.

What went wrong, and what is next

  • A text-only classifier reaches 86.8% winner accuracy on the same cases, beating the pipeline's 75.1%, so on winner accuracy alone the agents do not yet beat a simple baseline.
  • The pipeline's confidence score correlates only weakly with correctness (0.19), so it is a poor guide to when an output can be trusted.
  • The live AWS backend could not complete a single login until 14 September: the single-instance environment had no TLS listener and the frontend correctly refused plain HTTP. I fixed it with a CloudFront distribution in front of the backend.
  • There is deliberately no self-service data deletion yet. A case record doubles as an evidentiary audit trail, so what to purge is a legal decision, not one I should make in code.

Next: Validating the LLM judge against human labels, finishing the TypeScript migration of the frontend, and growing the evaluation set.
