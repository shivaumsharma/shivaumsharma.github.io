Case study · Agentic AI · Fintech

FinSight AI

An equity-research and paper-trading platform where every claim traces back to a live filing, a real quote or a computed valuation, including the claim that the model is not yet better than doing nothing.

Role
Designed and built end to end: agents, valuation, evaluation, frontend, infrastructure
When
2026, still in active development
Stack
Next.js, TypeScript, FastAPI, ChromaDB, FinBERT, Snowflake (optional), AWS + Terraform
Scale
1,000+ pytest tests, ~1,000-ticker backtest universe

The problem

Most AI investing tools are chatbots wrapped around a generic model's training data: confident, ungrounded, and unable to say why they said what they said.

The goal was a system where a user asks about any listed company and gets a sourced, institutional-style report, and where the system is honest about its own limits instead of hiding them.

What I built

An LLM planner chooses which tools a question needs (market data, live SEC EDGAR and NSE India filings via RAG, a from-scratch WACC, FCFF and DCF valuation with Monte Carlo, FinBERT sentiment). A shared context feeds a report writer, and every report is scored on grounding and citation coverage before display.

Around that sits a simulated paper-trading layer, a 31-signal factor scorecard, voice input, and a point-in-time backtesting harness. The API and infrastructure run on AWS, defined in Terraform.

Decisions that mattered

  1. Report one accuracy number, and make it a hard one

    An earlier "70% accuracy" figure turned out to measure only agreement with analyst consensus. I replaced it with a single canonical metric: 12-month forward directional accuracy against an Always-Buy baseline, shown on every report.

  2. Keep the signals that don't earn their place display-only

    The 31-signal scorecard and the ML valuation classifier are architecturally and test-enforced never to influence the recommendation until they beat the baseline.

  3. Drop approaches that measured worse, and say so

    A hand-labelled precision, NDCG and MRR evaluation showed a cross-encoder reranker was silently degrading retrieval, so I shipped raw retrieval. A fine-tuned embedding model also underperformed the baseline, so I declined to ship it.

Results

  • 38.6%12-month forward accuracy (N=1,907, 95% CI 36.4 to 40.8)
  • 58.4%Always-Buy baseline (95% CI 56.1 to 60.6)
  • 4×faster reports: 257s down to 65s
  • 65findings in a code audit, including an RCE fix

The model currently loses to doing nothing. Buy calls carry real signal (52.8 to 65% precision) while Sell calls do not (as low as 15.6%), and every tested window sits inside a rising market where Always-Buy is a strong baseline.

What went wrong, and what is next

  • The backtest caught two look-ahead data leaks and a class-imbalance trap: a classifier's 61% raw accuracy was just majority-class guessing.
  • A walk-forward portfolio backtest also lost to a no-signal baseline. I traced why instead of stopping at the loss: the scoring signal is real but weak (Information Coefficient +0.093), and 45% of the biggest single-period gainers had been rated Sell, which a Buy-only portfolio structurally excludes. A tilt strategy improved risk-adjusted return but did not close the gap, and I have reported it as unfinished.

Next: Hybrid retrieval (vector plus BM25), a Postgres migration, and further work on the Sell-side signal.
